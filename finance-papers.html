10 Papers That Built Modern Finance

Ten papers. Five Nobel Prizes. All free.

Everything from CAPM to your broker's execution algo traces back to these. Read the originals — not the LinkedIn summary of the originals.

Each paper below has an In Practice note and a copy-paste AI prompt so you can actually apply the idea, not just read about it.

Act I

The Foundation

What every finance interview quotes — the five papers that gave the industry its language: diversification, arbitrage, efficiency, beta.

01 Harry Markowitz · 1952

Portfolio Selection

ProvedRisk lives in the portfolio, not the stock — covariance is what you actually own.
MattersThe origin of diversification and every portfolio optimiser since.
CatchForty stocks that all fall together is still one bet.
Read the paper →
In practice

Build a mean-variance view of your own book. Don't ask "is this a good stock" — ask "what does adding this position do to my portfolio's covariance." A basket of 40 stocks that all fall together in a drawdown is still one bet, no matter how diversified it looks on paper.

Prompt to steal — paste into Claude/ChatGPT
I hold these positions: [list your tickers and weights]. Pull daily returns for each over the last 3 years, build the covariance matrix, and plot the mean-variance efficient frontier. Show me where my current portfolio sits relative to that frontier, and suggest one reallocation that would reduce my risk for the same expected return.
02 Franco Modigliani & Merton Miller · 1958

The Cost of Capital, Corporation Finance and the Theory of Investment

ProvedWith no frictions, debt vs equity does not change firm value.
MattersIt gave finance its method — state the perfect case, then hunt what breaks it.
CatchThe real world is nothing but frictions. The paper is famous for being wrong on purpose.
Read the paper →
In practice

When you're sizing up a company, don't over-weight its debt/equity mix in isolation. Ask what the capital-structure choice is actually doing to taxes, distress risk and cash flow — the real-world frictions MM assumed away on purpose, which is exactly where the useful analysis lives.

Prompt to steal — paste into Claude/ChatGPT
Pull [ticker]'s debt and equity issuance from its last 3 annual reports. Explain in plain English how its capital structure has changed, then estimate — given today's interest rate environment — whether its current leverage is helping or hurting its cost of capital.
03 William Sharpe · 1964

Capital Asset Prices: A Theory of Market Equilibrium under Conditions of Risk

ProvedOnly non-diversifiable risk gets paid. Expected return is linear in beta.
MattersCAPM is still the language of every valuation and cost of equity.
CatchSixty years of evidence say beta alone does not explain returns.
Read the paper →
In practice

Use beta as a starting point for the return you should demand from a stock relative to the market — not the whole answer. It's the common language every cost-of-equity number and DCF discount rate is built on, so you need to speak it even while doubting it.

Prompt to steal — paste into Claude/ChatGPT
Calculate the 3-year rolling beta of [ticker] against the S&P 500. Using CAPM with the current 10-year Treasury yield as the risk-free rate and a 5% equity risk premium, tell me what expected return the model implies — then compare that to the stock's actual realized return over the same period and explain the gap.
04 Eugene Fama · 1970

Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work

ProvedThe weak / semi-strong / strong framework for what is already in the price.
MattersEvery claim of edge is implicitly a claim about which form fails.
CatchThe joint-hypothesis problem — it can never be tested without also testing a return model.
Read the paper →
In practice

Before you trust an "edge," ask which form of efficiency it claims to beat — weak (chart patterns), semi-strong (public news) or strong (insider information). That single question tells you exactly how skeptical to be, and how much competition you're actually up against.

Prompt to steal — paste into Claude/ChatGPT
I think [describe your signal or strategy] might be a market edge. Classify it as exploiting weak-form, semi-strong-form or strong-form market inefficiency, then give me the 3 most likely reasons this edge either doesn't really exist or has already been arbitraged away by now.
05 Fischer Black & Myron Scholes · 1973

The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities

ProvedAn option can be replicated with stock and cash, so arbitrage forces its price.
MattersThat single idea built the entire derivatives market.
CatchIt assumes constant volatility. The volatility smile is the market disagreeing.
Read the paper →
In practice

Use it to sanity-check an option's price and to understand implied volatility — but remember its constant-volatility assumption is exactly what the market's volatility smile is telling you is wrong. The model is the baseline; the smile is the market's correction to it.

Prompt to steal — paste into Claude/ChatGPT
Pull the current option chain for [ticker]. Calculate the Black-Scholes theoretical price for the nearest-expiry at-the-money call and put, then compare that to the market's actual prices. Describe how implied volatility changes across strikes — the volatility smile — and explain in plain English what that tells us Black-Scholes gets wrong.
Where to start

If you only have twenty minutes: read Markowitz 1952, section I, and Grossman-Stiglitz 1980's introduction. Between them they frame the whole field.

Act II

What the Desk Actually Runs On

The five nobody puts on a syllabus — position sizing, why active management is allowed to exist, factors, momentum, and the maths behind every execution algo on the street.

06 John L. Kelly Jr. · 1956

A New Interpretation of Information Rate

ProvedThere is one bet size that maximises long-run growth.
MattersPosition sizing decides survival. Edge without sizing is a slow way to lose.
CatchFull Kelly is brutally volatile — most desks run a fraction of it.
Read the paper →
In practice

Before sizing any position, estimate your real edge honestly — win probability and payoff ratio, not vibes. Compute full Kelly, then size at a fraction of it. Most professional desks run a quarter to a half Kelly, because full Kelly is mathematically optimal but psychologically unbearable.

Prompt to steal — paste into Claude/ChatGPT
My historical win rate on [describe your strategy] is X% with an average win/loss payoff ratio of Y. Calculate the full Kelly position size as a percentage of capital, then simulate the equity curve difference between full Kelly, half Kelly and quarter Kelly sizing over my last 100 trades — I want to see the drawdown difference, not just the average return.
07 Sanford Grossman & Joseph Stiglitz · 1980

On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets

ProvedPerfectly efficient markets are impossible when information costs money.
MattersThis is the paper that says an active manager's job is allowed to exist.
CatchThe inefficiency must be exactly big enough to pay for the research, and no bigger.
Read the paper →
In practice

Use this as your reality check on any "free" signal. If a piece of information were truly costless to find and instantly priced in, nobody would be paid to look for it. So ask what it actually costs you — time, data, compute — to find your edge, and whether that cost is still worth paying.

Prompt to steal — paste into Claude/ChatGPT
Explain the Grossman-Stiglitz paradox applied to [describe your strategy or signal]. What would have to be true about the cost of finding this information for it to still be profitable after transaction costs? Give me a checklist I can use to judge whether this signal is likely already arbitraged away.
08 Eugene Fama & Kenneth French · 1993

Common Risk Factors in the Returns on Stocks and Bonds

ProvedSize and value explain what beta alone could not. The three-factor model.
MattersEvery factor fund, smart-beta product and risk model alive descends from this.
CatchFactors decay once published. Value spent a decade in the wilderness.
The 1993 original is paywalled at Elsevier. The link below is a different, later paper — Fama & French (2004), “The Capital Asset Pricing Model: Theory and Evidence,” Journal of Economic Perspectives. Same authors, same argument, open access, and a much better first read.
Read the 2004 open-access version →
In practice

Before calling a return "alpha," check whether it's actually just size or value factor exposure you haven't measured. Run your portfolio or backtest through a three-factor regression first — a lot of "skill" quietly disappears once you control for size and value.

Prompt to steal — paste into Claude/ChatGPT
Take the daily returns of [my portfolio or strategy] over the last 5 years. Run a Fama-French three-factor regression against the market, SMB (size) and HML (value) factors — Ken French's data library has the factor returns. Tell me my factor loadings and whether my alpha is still statistically significant after controlling for size and value.
09 Narasimhan Jegadeesh & Sheridan Titman · 1993

Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers: Implications for Stock Market Efficiency

ProvedBuying 12-month winners and shorting losers earned significant returns.
MattersMomentum survived publication, out-of-sample testing and four decades of attack.
CatchIt crashes violently, and exactly when you need it not to.
Read the paper →
In practice

A simple momentum sleeve — rank stocks by trailing 12-month return, skip the most recent month, hold the top decile — is one of the most replicated results in finance. Size it small: momentum crashes hard and fast, usually right when the broader market is turning.

Prompt to steal — paste into Claude/ChatGPT
Build a simple 12-month momentum backtest on S&P 500 constituents: rank by trailing 12-month return skipping the most recent month, go long the top decile, rebalance monthly. Show me the equity curve, max drawdown and the worst 3 individual months — I want to see the crash risk clearly, not just the average annual return.
10 Robert Almgren & Neil Chriss · 2000

Optimal Execution of Portfolio Transactions

ProvedTrade fast and pay impact, trade slow and carry risk — there is an optimal path.
MattersThe maths behind every VWAP, TWAP and execution algo on the street.
CatchImpact parameters are estimated, unstable and specific to your size.
Journal of Risk 3(2), Winter 2000/2001. The author's original NYU page is now offline — the link below is a verified 2017 Internet Archive capture of it.
Read the paper →
In practice

If you're ever trading a size the market can't absorb in one clip, don't just fire a market order. Think in terms of the tradeoff between market impact (trading fast) and price risk (trading slow) — that tradeoff is exactly what every VWAP and TWAP execution algo is automating for you.

Prompt to steal — paste into Claude/ChatGPT
I need to buy $[amount] of [ticker], which trades about $[average daily volume] a day. Using a simple Almgren-Chriss-style execution framework, suggest how I should split this order across the trading day to balance market impact against price risk, and compare that plan to just using a straight TWAP.

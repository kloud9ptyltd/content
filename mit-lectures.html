01 · MIT 15.S50 · Lecture 1

Poker Theory

FREE1.8M VIEWS30 MINNO MATHS

The first class of MIT's Poker Theory and Analytics course. PokerStars set up a private, play-money league just for the students, and trading firms put up the prizes: Akuna for the weekly tournaments and Optiver for the live final.

Taught by: Kevin Desmond.

Jump straight to
  • 0:45the private PokerStars league
  • 5:31why players who beat weak opponents get crushed when the game gets real
  • 7:02the prizes and the live final

Why it matters: Poker is the cleanest practice there is for betting when you cannot see everything, which is why trading firms like it.

Finance Theory

FREE2.9M VIEWS1 HR 7 MINNO MATHS

Day one of Andrew Lo's Finance Theory course for MIT Sloan students. He auctions a wrapped box that nobody is allowed to touch or shake. The class bids it up to $45. Inside was an iPod that sold for $149.

Taught by: Professor Andrew Lo.

Jump straight to
  • 13:27the maths professor who became the best-paid hedge fund manager of his year
  • 26:03the mystery box auction
  • 37:07what the $45 tells you about how markets price things

Why it matters: His point: a market will put a price on anything, even with almost no information, and the discount for the unknown is not crazy.

03 · MIT 18.S096 · Lecture 16

Portfolio Management

FREE6.1M VIEWS1 HR 29 MINSOME MATHS

From MIT's maths for finance course. The example from the reel: stock A doubles, then halves. Stock B halves, then doubles. Both end exactly where they started. Hold half of each and rebalance after year one, and the mix is up 25% in each year.

Taught by: A former Morgan Stanley trader.

Jump straight to
  • 2:46he gives the class five minutes to build a portfolio on a blank page
  • 51:42the two-stock example, "the closest thing to a free lunch"
  • 1:05:01why everyone using the same strategy is dangerous

Why it matters: The 25% comes from made-up numbers chosen to show the idea. Real stocks do not double and halve on schedule. The lesson is that mixing and rebalancing can beat holding either one.

Full course: Topics in Mathematics with Applications in Finance ↗

04 · MIT 18.S096 · Lecture 1

Financial Terms and Concepts

FREE8M VIEWS1 HRLIGHT MATHS

The opening lecture of the same course, and its most watched. He gives the room a choice: lose $280 for certain, or take a gamble with an 80% chance to lose $500 and a 20% chance to win $500. The gamble is worse on average, and some people still take it.

Taught by: Jake Xia, former Morgan Stanley options trader.

Jump straight to
  • 5:46his first day on a Wall Street options desk
  • 42:44why the "holy grail" trading strategy does not exist
  • 44:32the sure loss or the gamble

Why it matters: In that MIT room only about 6 of 50 took the gamble. He says a wider crowd would take it far more often, because people hate locking in a loss. That is the habit behind holding losing trades.

05 · MIT 18.S096 · Lecture 5

Stochastic Processes

FREE1.1M VIEWS1 HR 18 MINHEAVY MATHS

The maths of things that move at random, like a price. The game from the reel: bet $1 on a fair coin and keep playing until you are up $100 or down $50. You reach the $100 only one time in three.

Taught by: The course maths instructor (not named in the video).

Jump straight to
  • 23:22the coin game and the one-in-three answer
  • 1:02:24why roulette is built for the casino
  • 1:07:05no strategy can beat a fair game

Why it matters: A bigger target on a fair bet means you win less often, so the average stays at zero. By the same sum, a $200 target with a $50 stop wins one time in five. This is the hardest of the five lectures.

02 · Good to know

Honest notes.

Five lectures will not make you a quant. They show you how quants think about bets, prices and risk. The job itself takes years of maths, statistics and code.
These are full university classes. Most run an hour or more and they are not edited for entertainment. The jump links are there so you can see the best part first.
The 25% a year is a classroom example, built from made-up numbers to show why rebalancing works. Nobody is promising you that return.
The coin game is a fair bet with no edge. Real trading has costs, and most strategies have no edge either, which is the point of the lecture.
The lectures are from 2008 to 2015. The ideas have not changed, but some of the market examples and numbers are old.

01 · Terms 1–11

The basics

What you're actually buying, and where.

01

stock

A piece of a company

Ownership in a company. If the company grows and earns more, your piece is worth more. If it shrinks, so does your piece.

02

share

One unit of that stock

Stock is the ownership, a share is one unit of it. Own 10 shares of a company that has 1,000 shares and you own 1% of it.

03

ticker

The stock's short code

The short code a stock trades under. Apple is AAPL, Tesla is TSLA, Commonwealth Bank is CBA. It's what you type into your broker.

04

broker

Places your trades for you

The app or firm that sends your orders to the exchange. You can't trade on an exchange directly, so every trade goes through one.

05

exchange

Where buyers meet sellers

The marketplace where shares change hands, like the New York Stock Exchange, the Nasdaq or the ASX.

06

IPO

A company's first public sale

Initial public offering. The first time a private company sells its shares to the public. After that, anyone can buy and sell them on the exchange.

07

dividend

Profit paid out to shareholders

Cash a company pays its shareholders out of profit, usually every quarter or half-year. Not every company pays one. Many growing companies reinvest the money instead.

08

market cap

Share price × total shares

What the whole company is worth on the market. A $50 stock with 1 billion shares is a $50 billion company. The share price alone tells you nothing about size.

09

blue chip

Big, established, steady company

A large company with a long record of steady profits, like Microsoft or BHP. There's no official list. It's a label, not a guarantee.

10

index

A basket that tracks the market

A list of stocks used to measure the market. The S&P 500 tracks 500 of the largest US companies. When people say "the market is up", they mean an index.

11

ETF

A fund that trades like a stock

Exchange-traded fund. One ticker that holds many stocks. Buy an S&P 500 ETF and you own a small piece of all 500 companies in one trade.

02 · Terms 12–14

Market moods

The words the news uses when prices move.

12

bull market

Prices rising for months

A long stretch of rising prices. The common yardstick is a rise of 20% or more from a recent low.

13

bear market

Down 20% or more from the peak

A fall of 20% or more from a recent high. Bear markets are rarer than corrections and usually take longer to recover from.

14

correction

A 10% drop from the peak

A fall of 10% to 20% from a recent high. Corrections are a normal part of markets and happen far more often than bear markets.

03 · Terms 15–23

How a trade works

What happens between tapping buy and owning the shares.

15

bid

Highest price a buyer offers

The highest price any buyer is offering right now. If you sell immediately, this is roughly the price you get.

16

ask

Lowest price a seller accepts

The lowest price any seller will take right now. If you buy immediately, this is roughly the price you pay.

17

spread

The gap between bid and ask

Ask minus bid. If the bid is $10.00 and the ask is $10.02, the spread is 2 cents. It's a small hidden cost on every trade, and it gets wider in thinly traded stocks.

18

volume

How many shares traded

The number of shares traded in a period, usually one day. A big price move on high volume means a lot of traders took part in it.

19

liquidity

How easily you can get out

How easily you can buy or sell without moving the price. Big stocks are liquid. Small ones can be hard to exit at the price you see.

20

market order

Buy now at any price

Buy or sell right now at the best available price. You're sure to get filled, but you're not sure of the price.

21

limit order

Buy only at your price

Buy or sell only at your price or better. You're sure of the price, but the order might never fill.

22

stop loss

Auto-exit to cap your loss

An order that sells if the price falls to a level you set. Buy at $100 with a stop at $95 and your planned loss is about 5%. If the price gaps down overnight, the fill can be worse than your stop.

23

position size

How much you put in one trade

How much money goes into a single trade. A common rule of thumb is to size each trade so that hitting your stop costs only 1% to 2% of your account.

04 · Terms 24–27

Direction and borrowed money

How traders bet on a fall, and how accounts blow up.

24

long

Profit when the price rises

You buy, expecting the price to go up. The most you can lose is what you paid.

25

short

Profit when the price falls

You borrow shares, sell them, and buy them back later, hopefully cheaper. Because a price can keep rising, the loss on a short has no ceiling.

26

leverage

Trading with borrowed money

Using borrowed money to hold a bigger position than your cash allows. At 2x leverage, a 10% move in the stock is a 20% move in your money, in either direction.

27

margin call

Broker demands more cash

When a leveraged position falls too far, your broker asks for more cash. If you don't add it, they can sell your position for you, usually at a bad price.

05 · Terms 28–34

Reading a stock

The numbers and chart words you'll hear every day.

28

earnings

A company's quarterly profit report

The regular report of how much a company made. It's quarterly in the US and half-yearly in Australia. Stocks often jump or drop on the day, depending on how results compare with what was expected.

29

P/E ratio

Price ÷ earnings per share

Share price divided by earnings per share. A $100 stock that earns $5 a share has a P/E of 20, so you're paying $20 for every $1 of yearly profit.

30

volatility

How wildly the price moves

How much a price swings around, usually measured as the standard deviation of its returns. Higher volatility means bigger moves both ways.

31

drawdown

Fall from peak to bottom

The drop from a high point to the low that follows. An account that goes from $10,000 to $8,000 is in a 20% drawdown, and it needs a 25% gain to get back.

32

support

Where falling prices tend to stop

A price level where buyers have stepped in before, so falls have tended to pause there. It's a tendency, not a floor.

33

resistance

Where rising prices tend to stall

A price level where sellers have stepped in before, so rallies have tended to stall there. When price breaks through it, traders call that a breakout.

34

moving average

Average price over recent days

The average closing price over the last set number of days, like 50 or 200. It smooths out the daily noise so you can see the trend.

06 · Terms 35–37

Testing and protecting

What separates a plan from a guess.

35

backtest

Test a strategy on past data

Running a strategy's rules on historical prices to see how it would have done. A good backtest is a starting point, not proof it will work live.

36

slippage

Expected price vs the fill you got

The gap between the price you expected and the price you actually got. It's small in liquid stocks and larger in fast or thin markets, and it quietly eats into backtested returns.

37

hedge

Insurance against a loss

A second position that gains when your main one falls. Like insurance, it costs money and reduces your upside a little.

07 · Terms 38–41

Options

Contracts on a stock, not the stock itself.

38

options

Right to trade at a set price

Contracts that give you the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell a stock at a set price before a set date. You pay a premium for that right. One US contract usually covers 100 shares.

39

call

The right to buy

An option to buy at the set price. It gains value when the stock rises. If the stock never gets there, the most you lose is the premium.

40

put

The right to sell

An option to sell at the set price. It gains value when the stock falls, so investors often buy puts as insurance on shares they own.

41

implied volatility

Market's guess of future swings

The amount of future movement the market is pricing into an option. Higher implied volatility means pricier options. It often rises ahead of earnings.

08 · Terms 42–45

The quant end

How professionals measure a strategy.

42

beta

How much it swings vs the market

How sensitive a stock is to the market. A beta of 1.5 means it has tended to move about 1.5% for every 1% move in the market.

43

alpha

Returns above the market

The return left over after you account for the market. If the market made 10% and a fund with the same market exposure made 12%, the extra 2% is alpha.

44

Sharpe ratio

Return per unit of risk

Return above the risk-free rate, divided by volatility. It tells you how much return you got for the risk you took. Above 1 is generally considered good.

45

Monte Carlo simulation

Thousands of random possible futures

Running thousands of randomised scenarios to see the range of outcomes a portfolio or strategy could have, so you plan for a spread of results and not one forecast.

Your project

Load a year of daily stock prices into Python and compute daily returns.

  1. Open Google Colab (free, runs in your browser, nothing to install).
  2. Paste the prompt below into ChatGPT or Claude and run one cell at a time.
  3. Change the ticker to a stock you know and run it again.
Copy into ChatGPT or Claude
Teach me Python by building one small project in Google Colab. I've never coded.

Project: use yfinance to download 1 year of daily prices for AAPL. Load them into a pandas DataFrame, compute the daily return (today's close divided by yesterday's, minus 1), and print the average daily return, the best day and the worst day. Then plot the daily returns as a histogram.

Give me the code in small cells, one at a time, and explain every line like I'm 12. After each cell, ask me to run it and tell you what I see before moving on.

You're done when: you can see a table of daily returns and a histogram, and you can swap in a different ticker yourself.

Week 2 · Probability

Harvard Stat 110, Lecture 1: Probability and Counting

WatchHarvard Stat 110, Lecture 1: Probability and CountingJoe Blitzstein · 46 min · 1.8M viewsThe first lecture of Harvard's famous probability course. Counting problems like these are the base of quant interview questions.Watch free on YouTube ↗
Practice project · about 1 hour

Your project

Simulate 10,000 dice or coin games in Python and check the answer against the formula.

  1. Pick one simple game from the lecture, such as the chance of at least one six in four rolls.
  2. Paste the prompt below into ChatGPT or Claude and run the code in Google Colab.
  3. Compare the simulated answer with the exact formula.
Copy into ChatGPT or Claude
Help me learn probability by simulation. I just watched Lecture 1 of Harvard Stat 110 (Probability and Counting).

Game: roll one fair die 4 times. What is the probability of getting at least one six?
1. Explain how to work out the exact answer with a formula (using the complement).
2. Write beginner Python that simulates 10,000 games, then 1,000,000 games, and prints the simulated probability next to the formula answer.
3. Show how the simulation gets closer to the formula as the number of games grows.
4. Then give me 3 similar coin or dice problems to try myself, and check my answers when I reply.

Explain every line of code in plain English.

You're done when: your simulated answer lands within about 1% of the formula, and you can explain why the formula works.

Week 3 · Algo trading in Python

Algorithmic Trading Using Python - Full Course

Practice project · about 2 hours

Your project

Backtest a simple moving-average strategy against buy-and-hold, including trading costs.

  1. Open Google Colab and paste the prompt below into ChatGPT or Claude.
  2. Run it, then change the moving-average lengths and the cost, and watch the result change.
  3. Write one sentence on whether the strategy actually beat buy-and-hold.
Copy into ChatGPT or Claude
Act as my quant tutor. Using Python with yfinance and pandas, write a beginner-friendly backtest:

1. Download daily SPY prices from 2010 to today.
2. Strategy: hold SPY when the 50-day moving average is above the 200-day, otherwise hold cash. Use yesterday's signal to avoid look-ahead bias.
3. Charge 0.1% cost every time the position changes.
4. Compare with simply buying and holding SPY.

Print total return, annual return, max drawdown and number of trades for both, and plot the two equity curves.

Then explain in plain English why a backtest like this can mislead (overfitting, costs, look-ahead bias). This is research on past data only, not trading advice.

You're done when: you can say in one sentence whether the strategy beat buy-and-hold after costs, and why that result might not repeat.

Copy into ChatGPT or Claude
Act as a quant trading interviewer. I just watched Jane Street's public mock trading interview and want to practise.

Ask me one probability or expected-value question at a time, similar in style to a trading interview (for example a simple betting or dice game). Wait for my answer. Then tell me what I got right, where my reasoning was weak, and how a strong candidate would think out loud. Start easy and get slightly harder after each question. Do 5 questions, then summarise what to work on next.

You're done when: you have answered every question out loud before hearing the answer, and noted which ones you would do differently.

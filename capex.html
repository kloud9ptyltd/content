The Buildout
Public data · Goldman · NVIDIA · Columbia

The biggest buildout in history — $7.6 trillion.

Between 2026 and 2031, an estimated $7.6 trillion of AI compute capex — over $1T in 2026 alone. Measured against GDP, it's the largest capex-driven boom ever recorded. Here are the charts, and the part the charts don't say out loud.

The scale, in one line

AI at ~3.7% of GDP is nearly double the railroad boom — the prior record — and it dwarfs electrification, highways and the internet. Goldman now puts 2026 global AI investment above $1.019 trillion.

The rate problem — the money just got expensive

Financed at a 19-year high

The 30-year US Treasury yield hit 5.31% (Aug 17, 2026) — its highest since July 2007. When these AI capex plans were drawn up, borrowing was near-free (~1% in 2020). Now $7.6T of mostly debt-funded buildout is being financed at the priciest long-term rates in almost two decades. Higher-for-longer rates are exactly what stresses a leveraged capex boom.

The part the chart doesn't say out loud

The two next-biggest booms both busted

Railroads (1870s over-building) → mass bankruptcies. Telecom & fibre (1996–2003 dark-fibre glut) → the dot-com bust. The infrastructure got built and stayed — but much of the money that financed it did not survive.

Bubble or boom? Both.

▲ The boom is real

$7.6T of compute has to be physically built — chips, power and data centres are real assets that will exist and produce.

▼ The financing is the risk

Much is debt-funded against fast-depreciating GPUs. If demand digests, the leverage — not the buildings — is where it breaks.

◆ Different clocks

The economy gets built AND the money gets stress-tested — just not on the same timeline. Own the real layer; respect the leverage.

Cloud9's read — the TLDR

The easy money is already behind us

Here's our own view, said plainly: the generational entry point into the AI trade was 2-3 years ago. Nvidia has gone parabolic. Palantir ran from ~$7 to ~$170. Micron and SanDisk have gone astronomical. The obvious money has largely been made.

What's left is a market where almost everything is priced for perfection - years of flawless execution already baked into the price (Bloom Energy is a textbook example). That doesn't mean it ends tomorrow. It means the risk/reward has quietly flipped from "a rising tide lifts all boats" to "a stock-picker's and risk-manager's market."

Where we think we sit on the boom curve: somewhere in the 80-100% zone near the top - or just over it, beginning the slow grind down. Not the start. Not necessarily a crash. The late innings.

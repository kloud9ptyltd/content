02

What Microsoft just launched.

Chat models like ChatGPT or Claude write paragraphs. Microsoft-Decision-1 is different: you give it some data and a list of fixed answers, and it returns a probability for each answer. No essay, no "it depends". Just "trending 83%, choppy 13%, crisis 0%".

That makes it fast and very cheap, which is exactly what a bot that runs all day needs. It went live on OpenRouter on 10 October 2026. It's a drop-in swap for TypeSafe's Jev, the decision model that started this trend in September: same request, same answer format. My bot ran on Jev first, and switching to Microsoft was a one-line change that made each decision about 44% cheaper in my tests.

03

Rule #1: never ask an AI "should I buy?"

That's the mistake almost every AI trading bot makes. "Look at Bitcoin and tell me what to do" is not a question any model answers well. So the bot splits the work into four layers, and the AI only does the one thing it's good at: judgment calls.

Layer 1 · Code

Calculates

Every second: spread, order-book depth, who's buying vs selling, volatility, funding. Pure maths, no AI.

→
Layer 2 · AI

Judges

Microsoft-Decision-1 answers 9 narrow questions in one call. Is this a cascade? Are those walls fake?

→
Layer 3 · Code

Decides

My own rules turn the answers into an action. The thresholds live in my code, never in the model.

→
Layer 4 · Risk engine

Vetoes

Hard limits checked every time. It can overrule the AI, and the AI can never overrule it.

05

The trick: it only thinks when the market moves.

The code checks the market every second, but most seconds nothing happens. So instead of asking the AI 86,400 times a day, the bot only wakes it up when something real changes:

  • Price moved 5 basis points (0.05%) since the AI last looked
  • The buy/sell balance flipped near the price
  • Volatility spiked above its normal level
  • A burst of aggressive orders: 4× the usual pace, over $250k in 10 seconds
  • Big walls appeared and vanished (possible spoofing), or a hidden buyer or seller started absorbing trades
  • The order book drained below 60% of its normal depth
  • A 30-second heartbeat, so the screen never goes stale

06

The rules the AI can't touch.

The AI gives probabilities. My code decides what to do with them, checked in this order:

Policy · plain code
if cascade >= 0.70:                 GET OUT        # forced selling is snowballing
elif smart_money_flow >= 0.60:       GET OUT        # don't fight informed traders
elif thin_book >= 0.70:              CAUTION        # limit orders only
elif fake_walls >= 0.70:             CAUTION
elif conditions >= "Standard" and confidence >= 0.80
     and safe_to_trade >= "OK":      GREEN LIGHT
elif conditions >= "Marginal":       TRADE SMALL
else:                                SIT OUT
  • GREEN LIGHT

    Good conditions. Trade your normal size.

  • TRADE SMALL

    OK, not great. Smaller size, wider stops.

  • CAUTION

    Thin book or fake walls. Limit orders only, no market orders.

  • GET OUT

    Cascade or smart money moving. Cancel orders, close or tighten stops.

  • SIT OUT

    Nothing worth doing. No new trades.

  • EMERGENCY STOP

    The risk engine's kill switch. Everything off until it clears.

Then the risk engine gets the final word. These are hard limits that no AI answer can override:

  • Stale data → sit out

    If the market feed is more than 3 seconds old, it doesn't act on it.

  • Late answer → no trade

    If the AI takes longer than 2.5 seconds, that answer is thrown away.

  • Volatility spike → emergency stop

    Wild 30-second moves switch everything off for 60 seconds.

  • Feed disconnected → emergency stop

    No data, no decisions, until the feed is back.

  • AI down → code-only rules

    After 3 errors in a row it falls back to simple rules, and retries the AI every 60 seconds.

  • Every veto is logged

    On the live screen you'll see the AI's pick crossed out when the risk engine says no.

07

Build your own.

Here's what one decision call looks like. You need an OpenRouter account and API key. The Bitcoin order-book data comes free from Hyperliquid's public feed.

One call · Python
import json, os, urllib.request

body = {
  "model": "microsoft/microsoft-decision-1",
  "state": {"spread_bps": 0.4, "imbalance": 0.21, "vol_ratio": 1.2,
            "aggr_buy_10s": 16690, "aggr_sell_10s": 9840},
  "questions": {
    "regime": {"type": "choice", "instructions": "Current BTC market regime?",
               "criteria": {"trending": "Persistent one-way move",
                            "mean_reverting": "Oscillating around a level",
                            "high_vol": "Large moves both ways",
                            "crisis": "Disorderly, liquidity vanishing"}},
    "cascade": {"type": "noul", "instructions": "Is a liquidation cascade starting?",
                "criteria": {"true": "Cascade starting", "false": "No cascade"}},
  },
}
req = urllib.request.Request(
  "https://openrouter.ai/api/alpha/decisions",
  data=json.dumps(body).encode(), method="POST",
  headers={"Authorization": "Bearer " + os.environ["OPENROUTER_API_KEY"],
           "Content-Type": "application/json"})
print(json.loads(urllib.request.urlopen(req).read())["answers"])

08

Turning it into a real trading bot.

The decision engine is not a strategy. It's a traffic light. A real bot needs two parts: a strategy that decides what to trade, and this engine deciding when it's safe. If you want to take it live, this is the order I'd do it in.

  1. 1

    Start with a strategy that works without the AI. Something simple and testable, like a moving-average trend follower or a breakout on the 15-minute chart. Backtest it with fees and slippage included. If it loses money on its own, the AI filter won't rescue it.

  2. 2

    Use the signal as a filter, not a trigger. The strategy finds the trade; the signal decides whether it's allowed and how big. See the table below.

  3. 3

    Build on testnet first. Hyperliquid runs a full test exchange with fake money (api.hyperliquid-testnet.xyz) and an official Python SDK (hyperliquid-python-sdk). Get every order type working there before real money exists anywhere in your code.

  4. 4

    Lock down the keys. Trade through an API wallet: it can place orders but can't withdraw funds. Fund a separate sub-account with only what you can afford to lose. Keep keys in environment variables or a secret manager, never in your code.

  5. 5

    Size every trade by risk, not by gut. Risk 0.5–1% of the account per trade: position size = (account × risk %) ÷ distance to your stop. Use isolated margin and cap leverage at 2–3×.

  6. 6

    Put the stop-loss on the exchange, not in your bot. Send a stop-loss trigger order together with every entry. If your bot crashes, loses internet or freezes, the stop still protects you.

  7. 7

    Add account-level limits in code. A daily loss limit (for example −3%, then no more trades that day), a maximum position size, and a cap on orders per minute. Also switch on Hyperliquid's dead-man's switch (scheduleCancel): if your bot stops checking in, the exchange cancels your open orders for you.

  8. 8

    Treat the exchange as the truth. On start-up, and every minute after, read your real positions and open orders from the exchange and fix any mismatch. Never trust what the bot thinks it holds.

  9. 9

    Make fees part of the maths. Prefer post-only limit orders (Hyperliquid's "Alo" order type), which pay the lower maker fee. If a strategy's average profit per trade isn't comfortably above the round-trip fees, it isn't a strategy yet.

  10. 10

    Prove the AI earns its place. Paper-trade the strategy with and without the filter, side by side, for 2–4 weeks. Keep the filter only if it actually cuts losing trades or drawdowns.

  11. 11

    Go live tiny. Start with $50–100 for two weeks. Read the logs every few days. Scale up slowly, and only after the live results match the paper results.

09

What this is, and what it isn't.

  • It's a decision engine, running on paper. No real orders, no real money. It shows what the AI would do and why, live, second by second.
  • It's not a money printer. Fast, cheap judgments don't create an edge by themselves. Finding an edge is still the hard part, and fees eat most naive strategies alive.
  • It's not Wall Street HFT. Professional firms trade in microseconds on custom hardware. A bot that thinks in about half a second has no business competing with them.
  • Break it on purpose. Cut the network, slow the API, breach a limit, and check the bot recovers by itself. A fallback that never retries the AI will quietly leave you on dumb rules.
  • Test before you trust. Run it on paper for weeks. Check whether the AI's 80% really happens 80% of the time on your market, and whether the fees beat the profit per trade.

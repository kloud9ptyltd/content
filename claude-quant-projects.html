01 · Monte Carlo simulator

Stress-test a portfolio across 10,000 futures.

Teaches · Probability

Instead of guessing one future, you simulate thousands of possible ones and read the spread of outcomes. It's the first thing quant interviews probe: expected value, distributions, percentiles and the law of large numbers.

Paste into Claude
Build a Monte Carlo portfolio simulator in Python.

1. Download SPY's daily prices from 2006 to today with yfinance.
2. Simulate 10,000 one-year paths (252 trading days) for a $100,000 investment by bootstrapping: resample real daily returns at random, with replacement.
3. Report the median, 5th percentile and 95th percentile ending values, and the probability of ending with less than $100,000.
4. Plot a fan chart (100 sample paths, the 5–95% band and the median) and a histogram of the 10,000 ending values.
5. Let me pass any portfolio as tickers and weights (for example 60% SPY, 40% TLT) and compare it with 100% SPY.

Put it all in one file I can run with: python monte_carlo.py
Explain each step in plain English as you go, including why bootstrapping real returns is better than assuming a normal distribution.

What ours showed · $100K in the S&P 500, one year

$81K
Bad year (5th percentile)
$111K
Typical year (median)
$153K
Great year (95th percentile)
29%
Chance of ending the year down

Bootstrapped from 5,236 real S&P 500 daily returns (Jan 2006 to Oct 2026). Yours will differ by a few hundred dollars because every run draws new random paths. That's the point.

02 · Backtester

Catch an overfit strategy before it costs you.

Teaches · Overfitting

Anyone can tune a strategy until it looks great on old data. A real backtester tests it on data it has never seen. If the results collapse, the strategy was memorising noise. Interviewers love this topic because most people get it wrong.

Paste into Claude
Build a backtester in Python for a moving-average crossover on SPY: hold SPY when the fast moving average is above the slow one, hold cash when it's below.

1. Use SPY daily prices from 2015 to today (yfinance). Act on the NEXT day after a signal, so there's no lookahead.
2. Split the data: 2015–2020 is in-sample, 2021–today is out-of-sample.
3. Search fast/slow pairs (fast 5–50, slow 20–200) on the in-sample period only, and pick the best by Sharpe ratio.
4. Run that exact pair, unchanged, on the out-of-sample period.
5. For both periods, report Sharpe ratio, CAGR, max drawdown and number of trades, next to simply buying and holding SPY.
6. Include 0.05% cost per trade.
7. Plot both equity curves with the split marked, plus a drawdown chart.

Then tell me honestly: is this strategy overfit? Explain how you can tell.

What ours showed · the best in-sample pair (15/60 days)

1.02
Sharpe in-sample, beating buy-and-hold's 0.74
0.61
Sharpe on unseen data, where buy-and-hold got 0.91
6.5%
Yearly return out-of-sample, vs 14.8% for buy-and-hold
−24%
Worst drawdown out-of-sample, double the in-sample −13%

Our run used no trading costs, so yours (with 0.05%) should look slightly worse. The lesson holds either way: the "winning" strategy lost to doing nothing once it met new data.

03 · Pairs-trading bot

Trade Coke against Pepsi.

Teaches · Stat arb

Coca-Cola and PepsiCo sell similar things to the same people, so their prices tend to move together. When they drift unusually far apart, a pairs trader bets on the gap closing: sell the one that ran ahead, buy the one that lagged. This is mean reversion, the idea behind statistical arbitrage.

Paste into Claude
Build a pairs-trading research tool in Python for Coca-Cola (KO) and PepsiCo (PEP).

1. Download 6 years of daily prices for both with yfinance.
2. Regress log(KO) on log(PEP) to get the hedge ratio, and run an Engle-Granger cointegration test on the spread. Explain the result in plain English.
3. Compute a rolling 60-day z-score of the spread.
4. Trading rules: when z goes above +2, sell KO and buy PEP. When z goes below −2, do the reverse. Exit when z crosses back through 0.
5. Backtest it with 0.05% cost per trade, list every trade (dates, entry z, exit z, profit) and the total.
6. Plot both prices, the z-score with the ±2 bands, and every entry and exit.

Finish with: when does a pairs trade break, and how would a real desk manage that risk?

What ours showed · KO vs PEP, Oct 2020 to Oct 2026

16
Times the gap stretched past 2σ in six years
3.1σ
Peak divergence, Nov 2025: sell KO, buy PEP
34 days
From entry (6 Nov 2025) until the gap closed (10 Dec 2025)

Price correlation 0.67, hedge ratio 0.48. The spread itself moved less than 1% on that trade, which is why real desks run many pairs at once and use leverage. One pair on its own is a lesson, not a business.

04 · Factor model

Find out what really drives returns.

Teaches · Regression

A factor model splits a stock's returns into exposures to well-known drivers: the overall market, cheap "value" stocks, profitable "quality" companies, recent winners ("momentum") and more. It's how institutional funds decide where billions go, and it's the regression you'll be asked to explain.

Paste into Claude
Build a factor model in Python.

1. Download the monthly Fama-French 5 factors and the momentum factor from Kenneth French's data library (the CSV zip files on his website).
2. Download monthly returns for Berkshire Hathaway (BRK-A) from 1990 to today with yfinance.
3. Regress Berkshire's excess return (over the risk-free rate) on Market, Size (SMB), Value (HML), Profitability (RMW), Investment (CMA) and Momentum.
4. Show each factor's loading, its t-statistic and the R², and explain in plain English what they say about Warren Buffett's style.
5. Plot the loadings as a bar chart, highlighting which ones are statistically significant (|t| > 2).
6. Let me swap in any ticker, or my own portfolio, and rerun it.

What ours showed · Berkshire Hathaway, 1990–2026

+0.72
Market (t = 12.4): less swing than the market
+0.52
Value (t = 5.4): buys cheap companies
+0.23
Quality (t = 2.4): favours profitable ones
−0.02
Momentum (t = −0.5): no effect, doesn't chase trends

R² 0.36, from 1990-02 to 2026-09. Size came out at −0.36 (big companies) and Investment at −0.10 (not significant). Buffett's style, in numbers: big, cheap, profitable companies, without trend-chasing.

05 · Options pricer

Build Black-Scholes from scratch.

Teaches · Derivatives

Black-Scholes prices an option from five inputs, and the "Greeks" tell you how that price moves when each input changes. Every market maker's risk system is built on these numbers, which is why trading-firm interviews ask you to derive and explain them.

Paste into Claude
Build a Black-Scholes options pricer in Python from scratch (only numpy and scipy.stats; no options libraries).

1. Price European calls and puts, and compute delta, gamma, vega and theta.
2. Check my prices with put-call parity, and against a Monte Carlo price using 100,000 simulated paths.
3. Make a chart with sliders for stock price, days to expiry and volatility, showing the option's value curve against its payoff at expiry.
4. Pull a real option chain for SPY with yfinance, back out the implied volatility for each strike, and plot the volatility smile.

Explain each Greek with a simple example a beginner can follow, and why theta speeds up as expiry gets closer.

What ours showed · at-the-money call, strike $100, volatility 25%, rate 4.5%

$5.49 → $3.04
Option value, 90 days left vs 30 days left
0.56 → 0.53
Delta: moves about half as much as the stock
0.032 → 0.055
Gamma: rises sharply near expiry
−$0.03 → −$0.05
Theta: what the option loses each day

Same stock price, same volatility. Only time changed, and the option lost 45% of its value. That's time decay, and it speeds up as expiry gets closer.

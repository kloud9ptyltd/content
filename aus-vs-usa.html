The gap, explained in one line

Banks + miners ≈ half the ASX. Tech + the AI economy ≈ half the S&P. The last decade belonged to AI, cloud and software — Australia's index simply doesn't own much of it (~3–4% tech). You weren't picking countries; you were picking industries.

The currency angle most people miss

💱 US investing doubles as a currency hedge

An unhedged US ETF pays you in US dollars. Over the past decade the Aussie dollar slid from ~78¢ to ~65¢ US — so AUD investors in US assets got the market's return plus a currency kicker. And because so much of what Australians buy (tech, travel, fuel, iPhones) is priced in USD, holding USD assets hedges your own cost of living.

⚖️ …but it cuts both ways

If the AUD strengthens, unhedged US returns shrink for an Aussie holder. That's why both hedged and unhedged versions of the big US ETFs exist on the ASX. All-Australia portfolios have zero currency diversification — 100% AUD exposure is itself a bet.

A note on access (education, not a recommendation)

US index exposure is available through Australian brokers via ASX-listed versions of the same indexes — convenient, but the fees run higher than the US-listed originals. Holding the US-listed ETFs directly in USD (via an international broker such as IBKR) keeps the lower fee and the full currency-hedge effect described above. Each route has its own tax and paperwork trade-offs — do your own research.

The 30-year twist nobody expects

Split the 30 years in half and the story flips: from 1996 to about 2016, Australia was WINNING in USD terms — the commodities supercycle plus dodging the worst of the dot-com bust and the GFC beat America's "lost decade." The entire gap you see today was built in the last ten years — the AI, cloud and software era that Australia's index barely owns.

What the Nasdaq number really costs

That ≈$460K came with a price: the Nasdaq-100 fell ~80% in 2000–02 and took about 15 years to reclaim its dot-com high. Almost nobody held through that. The highest 30-year return belonged to the index that was hardest to keep holding — that's the real lesson, not the number.

The honest other side

⚠ What these numbers don’t tell you

1) The past decade is not a forecast. The S&P now trades near double the ASX's valuation, and the whole world is already crowded into US stocks (see The Flow below). Winners rotate.

2) Franking credits are real. Australian dividends carry tax credits that can add ~1.3–1.5%/yr after tax for local investors — none of which shows in these charts.

3) Neither index is a hedge for the other. Half the S&P is a bet on tech/AI staying dominant — but don't assume the ASX protects you if that bet fails. An AI bust would be a global-growth shock, and miners are levered to global demand (and China) — commodity stocks fall in worldwide downturns too. Australia's 2000s outperformance needed the China commodities boom to show up; there's no equivalent boom queued today. The honest framing: the S&P is concentrated in one new theme, the ASX in two old ones (bank credit + commodity cycles) — different concentrations, not diversification.

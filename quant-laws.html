The market is a game of survival, not prediction. The quants who last don't predict harder — they master the math, respect costs, and manage risk. This handbook is the map: what to learn, what to read, what to build, and the laws to live by.

Part 1 · The Roadmap

The 8 subjects quant is built on

Quant finance is mathematics first — not Python, not dashboards. Master these eight, and the code is the easy part.

01

Probability Theory

The language of uncertainty — the foundation of every pricing and risk model.

Start: Blitzstein & Hwang, Introduction to Probability (Harvard Stat 110 — free lectures on YouTube)
02

Statistics

Telling a real effect from a lucky streak — estimation and hypothesis testing.

Start: MIT 18.650 Statistics for Applications (free OCW) · Casella & Berger, Statistical Inference
03

Linear Algebra

The engine of portfolios — covariances, factors, PCA, and risk decomposition.

Start: Gilbert Strang, MIT 18.06 (free OCW) · 3Blue1Brown, Essence of Linear Algebra
04

Optimization

Building the best portfolio for the risk you take — under real constraints.

Start: Boyd & Vandenberghe, Convex Optimization (free PDF + Stanford EE364A lectures)
05

Machine Learning

Finding structure in data too complex to hand-code — and not overfitting it.

Start: Andrew Ng, Machine Learning (free audit) · Hastie et al., Elements of Statistical Learning (free PDF)
06

Numerical Methods

Solving the models that have no closed-form answer — root-finding and simulation.

Start: MIT 18.330 Numerical Analysis (free OCW) · Numerical Recipes
07

Random Processes

How prices evolve through time under continuous uncertainty.

Start: Sheldon Ross, Introduction to Probability Models · MIT 6.262 (free OCW)
08

Stochastic Calculus

Calculus for randomness — the math behind Black-Scholes and derivatives pricing.

Start: Steven Shreve, Stochastic Calculus for Finance I & II (the standard)
Part 2 · The Wisdom

The 12 Laws of Quant Trading

The principles every quant eventually learns — not from textbooks, from the tape.

LAW 01

Every model is wrong. A few are useful — briefly.

A model is a compression of reality, and compression always throws information away. Treat every model as a hypothesis with an expiry date, never a truth.

LAW 02

Reality is always messier than the mathematics.

Markets are people, incentives, liquidity and reflexivity — none of which fit a closed-form equation. When your model and the market disagree, the market is right.

LAW 03

Risk is what hasn't happened yet.

Volatility measures the past. Real risk is the fat-tailed event your sample never contained. Size for the drawdown you haven't seen.

LAW 04

The market pays you to bear uncertainty, not to be right.

Returns are compensation for holding risk others won't. The edge is in the risk premium, not the prediction.

Backtest vs Live
LAW 05

A backtest measures the past. It cannot promise the future.

A backtest is one draw from a distribution. Overfit it and you're memorising history. The only honest backtest is out-of-sample and paranoid about lookahead.

Signal Decay
LAW 06

Every edge decays the day it's discovered.

An edge exists because it isn't arbitraged yet. Once it's known, the return-to-risk erodes. Alpha is a depleting resource.

Net of Costs
LAW 07

Costs and slippage quietly kill most "profitable" strategies.

Gross returns are a fantasy. Commissions, spread, impact and taxes scale with turnover — a 20% gross strategy can be flat after costs.

LAW 08

Capacity is finite — size destroys the best edges.

The sharpest edges live in small, illiquid corners. The best ones are often too small to matter to a large fund — which is why they persist.

Assumptions vs Robustness
LAW 09

The more parameters a model needs, the less it survives out-of-sample.

Every free parameter is a degree of freedom to fit noise. Prefer the boring, robust model over the intricate, perfect one.

Correlation Matrix
LAW 10

Correlation isn't diversification — it vanishes when you need it most.

Uncorrelated assets converge toward 1 in a crisis. Real diversification comes from different risk drivers, not a historical matrix.

LAW 11

Position sizing matters more than the signal.

Same signal, opposite outcomes, depending on sizing. The signal decides direction; sizing decides survival.

Equity Curve
LAW 12

Risk management is the only alpha that compounds.

Returns are noisy and edges decay, but not blowing up is permanent. In the long run, staying alive is the strategy.

Part 3 · The Bookshelf

Best books & resources

Options, Futures & Other Derivatives — John Hull
The industry bible for derivatives and pricing.
Stochastic Calculus for Finance I & II — Steven Shreve
The standard path from random processes to Black-Scholes.
Active Portfolio Management — Grinold & Kahn
Alpha, risk, and the fundamental law of active management.
Advances in Financial Machine Learning — López de Prado
How to apply ML to markets without fooling yourself.
Quantitative Trading — Ernest Chan
A practical, honest intro to building and testing strategies.
A Practical Guide to Quant Finance Interviews — Xinfeng Zhou
The "green book" — brainteasers, probability, and the interview map.
Part 4 · Prove It

Projects to build

Nothing signals "hire me" like a public portfolio of working projects.

1 · Backtest a momentum + mean-reversion strategy
With realistic costs and slippage — prove it survives out-of-sample.
2 · Build a Markowitz portfolio optimizer
Trace the efficient frontier and find the max-Sharpe portfolio.
3 · Price a European option two ways
Black-Scholes vs Monte Carlo — and explain why they agree.
4 · Build a pairs-trading cointegration scanner
Find and test statistically-linked pairs across a universe.
5 · Run a PCA / factor model on a basket of stocks
Compress the market into its few real drivers of risk.
6 · Construct an implied-volatility surface
From real options data — the quant's favourite 3D chart.
Part 5 · The Path

How to break in

1
Master the math. Work through the 8 subjects above — depth beats breadth.
2
Code it. Python + NumPy, pandas and SciPy. Turn the math into working projects.
3
Prove it. A public portfolio of notebooks and honest, out-of-sample backtests.
4
Practice interviews. Probability brainteasers and the green book, until they're reflex.
5
Target the role. Quant Researcher, Quant Trader, or Quant Developer — pick your lane.

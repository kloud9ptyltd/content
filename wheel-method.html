Get paid to buy stocks

This is the guide from the reel. The Wheel is a simple loop: I get paid to promise to buy a stock cheaper, and if I end up owning it, I get paid again to promise to sell it higher. Below: how it works, a calculator with real option prices, and every risk, spelled out.

01

The idea in 30 seconds.

Lots of people who own NVDA are scared of a crash, so they buy insurance. That insurance is a put option: a promise from someone else to buy their shares at a set price, before a set date. When I sell that promise, they pay me upfront, like an insurance company collecting a premium.

At the 29 Sep 2026 close, NVDA traded at about $228. A promise to buy 600 shares at $180 (21% lower) by December 2028 paid $12,540 upfront.

02

The Wheel, step by step.

  1. 1

    Pick a company you'd happily own for years. This is the whole game. If you wouldn't buy it at the discount price anyway, stop here.

  2. 2

    Pick your discount price (the strike) and a deadline (the expiry). Lower strike = safer but pays less. Longer deadline = pays more but ties up your cash longer.

  3. 3

    Sell a cash-secured put. Keep strike × 100 × contracts in cash, so you can always afford the shares. The premium lands in your account straight away.

  4. 4

    Stock stays above your strike? The promise expires. You keep the premium, never buy anything, and can sell another put.

  5. 5

    Stock drops below your strike? You're "assigned": you buy the shares at the strike. Your real cost is strike minus the premium you already got.

  6. 6

    Now sell a covered call. A promise to sell your shares at a higher price. Pick a strike at or above your real cost, so if the shares get sold, it's never at a loss. You get paid again.

  7. 7

    Shares get sold? Back to step 1. Not sold? Keep the shares and the premium, and sell another call. That loop is the Wheel.

04

The full risk breakdown.

The reel shows the upside. Here's everything else, using the same NVDA trade.

  • You need the cash. 6 contracts at $180 means keeping $108,000 ready until December 2028. That money can't be used for anything else.
  • A crash still costs you. NVDA fell about 66% in 2022. If it drops to $120, I still pay $180 a share: that's a loss of about $23,460 even after keeping the premium.
  • You cap your upside. If NVDA doubles, a put seller only keeps the premium. A covered call seller has to sell at the call strike, however high the stock goes.
  • You can be assigned early. US stock options can be exercised any time before the deadline, not only on the final day.
  • Margin makes it riskier. Some traders secure puts with their other stocks instead of cash. If markets fall, the broker can demand more money or sell your positions.
  • Taxes and fees. Premiums are usually taxed as income, and bid/ask spreads on long-dated options can be wide. Check the rules where you live.
05

My checklist before the first trade.

  • ✓
    I'd buy this company today anyway.

    A high premium on a stock I don't want is a trap, not a bonus.

  • ✓
    The cash is set aside.

    Strike × 100 × contracts, in cash, not borrowed.

  • ✓
    I'm happy at the effective price.

    Strike minus premium is my real buy price. I'd be pleased to own it there.

  • ✓
    No earnings surprise inside a short deadline.

    For short-dated puts, check the next earnings date first.

  • ✓
    Calls only at or above my real cost.

    So getting my shares sold is never a forced loss.

The Repricing Map

AI erased $285 billion of SaaS in 24 hours. Here's the decade map.

Feb 3, 2026 — the SaaSpocalypse: Salesforce, ServiceNow, HubSpot, Infosys and Wipro shed ~$285B of market value in a single day. Not on earnings. On a realization: AI agents do the work, but they don't buy seats. This is Cloud9's full read — what's happening now, the 5-year mechanics, the 10–20 year endgame, and the winners & losers map. Every number is from filings, earnings calls, and our own fundamentals database.

01

What's happening right now

2025–2026
The mechanism — why per-seat pricing breaks
Per-seat pricing bills a proxy: humans logged in. Agents collapse the seat count while the work still gets done — and ~73% of SaaS companies are now rebuilding their pricing around usage or outcomes. In the transition, every renewal is a renegotiation where the buyer's alternative ("we'll have an agent build it") gets more credible each quarter. The alternative doesn't have to win to compress your price. It just has to be credible.
The street-level signal that front-ran Wall Street

Before Feb 3, the signal was already everywhere on social: an endless stream of "here's a free open-source version of [paid app]" videos — video editors, voice cloning, design tools, accounting — each one a tiny referendum on a subscription. Creators were documenting the price umbrella collapsing at the $30/month tier months before the market repriced the $30B tier. Social trends translate into stock moves — the lag is the opportunity.

The case study pair — capture vs. reprice

Canva went AI-native early — Magic Studio, 800M AI tool uses a month (+700%) — and ended 2025 at $4B ARR, 265M monthly users (+47%), enterprise segment doubling, with AI chatbots now referring it traffic. Figma stayed a seat-priced design tool — still growing +46% — and trades 80% below its high. Same disruption, opposite outcomes: AI-native incumbents capture the wave; seat-clingers get repriced by it. HubSpot is the third data point: after its pivot to agentic, consumption-based pricing it re-accelerated to +20% growth — the market hasn't forgiven it yet, but the P&L answered.

02

The next 5 years — the mechanics

Cloud9 thinking · 2026–2031

1 · The damage hits growth and multiples before gross margin. Software COGS is tiny — an 80% gross margin doesn't compress first. What compresses is price at renewal (slower revenue) and sales efficiency (every deal fights a credible agent-built alternative). Vendors will use the same AI to cut their own support and engineering costs, so reported margins hold deceptively long while growth bleeds out. Watch net revenue retention, not gross margin — NRR is where this shows up first.

2 · The renewal cycle is the clock. Enterprise contracts reprice on 1–3 year cycles. No cliff, no overnight churn — a grind, one renegotiation at a time, in only one direction. The threat does the work: like generics against branded pharma, the alternative doesn't need to win to reset the price.

3 · Self-hosting goes mainstream through agents, not through GitHub. Normies will never clone a repo — they won't have to. An agent installs, maintains, and monitors the open-source stack for them. The technical moat of "it's hard to run software" evaporates for exactly the customers who paid the highest markup for convenience: solopreneurs and small businesses at the $30–$300/month tier.

4 · Consulting already ran this experiment. Accenture: growth down to 3–4%, Q3 bookings negative, 784K people restructured around AI — while its AI bookings double. The accountability business survives; the price of accountability collapses. SaaS follows the same curve. Businesses do still buy liability and uptime — but AI resets what liability and uptime cost.

5 · Total software spend goes UP. The paradox that resolves everything: cheaper software means more software (the Jevons effect — same reason cheaper compute exploded compute demand). More software running means more compute, more storage, more networking, more monitoring, more incidents. The pie grows while the per-seat slice shrinks — which is why the infrastructure layer wins in both scenarios.

03

The 10–20 year endgame

Where value settles

Long enough out, application software stops being a product you buy and becomes something agents assemble on demand — ephemeral, personal, disposable. In that world, durable value can only settle where AI can't replicate the asset by writing code:

Physics — fabs, memory, power

Chips must be fabricated, memory must be stacked, electrons must be generated. TSMC's monopoly, the HBM oligopoly (three companies on earth supply it — hyperscalers are literally raising capex guidance "due to memory costs"), and the grid that powers inference. Code cannot compete with physics.

Networks — the edges nobody can rebuild

You cannot self-host a global edge network, a payment rail, or a marketplace's liquidity. Network effects are the one software moat agents don't dissolve — they may strengthen them, since agents need trusted rails to transact on.

Data gravity — systems of record

The ledger is the moat, not the interface on top of it. Payroll, ERP, health records, compliance-critical accounting: regulated data with liability attached migrates last, if ever. The interface layer above these gets rebuilt by agents; the record itself doesn't move.

Intelligence itself — models and their landlords

Every self-hosted stack, every agent-built app, every in-house CRM still rents intelligence per token. Model providers and the hyperscalers that host them tax the entire transition — whichever side of the SaaS war wins.

The 20-year question isn't "does SaaS die" — it's which layer of the stack captures the value that seats used to capture. Our answer: it flows down (to compute, memory, power, networks) and up (to models and agent platforms), and evaporates from the middle.

04

The winners & losers map

High level · 5–20 yr horizon

KILL ZONE — thin per-seat tools

Single-workflow, prosumer-tier apps (the $30/month tier): template design tools that stayed seat-priced, consumer video/photo editors, voice tools, basic bookkeeping front-ends, form builders, simple schedulers. Short distance from prompt to replacement. Switching cost was the moat — AI deletes switching cost.

COMPRESSION ZONE — horizontal mid-market SaaS

CRM-lite, project management, docs, e-sign, marketing suites. They survive, smaller — every renewal reprices against the agent alternative. The exit is real but narrow: convert seats to consumption and go AI-native fast (HubSpot and Canva prove it's capturable). The ones that can't convert become the value traps of the 2030s: profitable, shrinking, cheap forever.

HOLDS & WINNERS — moats not made of code

Compute & clouds: the hyperscalers with fortress balance sheets (watch relative capex exposure — debt-financed AI buildouts get punished). The chokepoints: HBM memory and leading-edge fabrication — cyclical, so entry price matters. Networks: the global edge, payment rails, marketplaces. Observability: millions of new agent-built stacks all need monitoring — the AI labs themselves buy it rather than build it. Data platforms: where the records and embeddings actually live. AI-native incumbents: the application-layer survivors who repriced themselves before the market did it for them.

What would prove us wrong — the falsifiers
  • Agent reliability plateaus — if agents stay 95%-good, businesses keep paying for the missing 5% in vendor form, and the compression stalls at the prosumer tier.
  • Compliance walls hold — regulators could effectively mandate vendor software in finance, health, and government, capping the migration.
  • The AI capex cycle busts — a capex digestion phase hits the "winners" hardest; the infrastructure trade is crowded and priced near highs.
  • Open source eats the winners too — the same logic that kills per-seat SaaS attacks observability (Grafana) and databases (Postgres); the survivors must keep out-executing free.
Honesty box: the easy repricing already happened on Feb 3, 2026 — this is a decade thesis, not a trade.

01 · Practice these strategies

To master quant trading.

Every strategy below is a rule a computer can follow. Practise each one the same way: write the rule, backtest it with fees, and write one sentence on why it worked or didn't.

  1. Momentum

    Buy what has gone up most over the last 3–12 months, sell what has fallen most. Winners tend to keep winning for a while. Task: rank 20 stocks by 12-month return each month.

  2. Mean reversion

    Bet that a price stretched far from its average snaps back. Task: buy when price is 2 standard deviations below its 20-day average.

  3. Trend following

    Go with the direction of the market and cut losers fast. Moving-average crossovers are the classic version. Task: 50/200-day crossover on an index.

  4. Breakout

    Enter when price breaks out of a recent range, on the idea that a new move has started. Task: buy a 20-day high, exit on a 10-day low.

  5. Pairs trading

    Find two stocks that move together (like Coke and Pepsi). When the gap between them widens, bet it closes. Task: test the textbook pair, KO/PEP, for cointegration (see list 04). The result may surprise you.

  6. Statistical arbitrage

    Pairs trading scaled up: hundreds of small, related bets that are each slightly mispriced, with the market risk hedged out.

  7. Market making

    Quote a price to buy and a price to sell at the same time and earn the gap (the spread). The risk is getting run over when the price moves.

  8. Carry

    Get paid for holding something: higher interest rates in currencies, funding rates in crypto, roll yield in futures. Task: chart a crypto funding rate for a month.

  9. Factor investing

    Tilt a portfolio towards traits that have paid over long periods, like value, quality, size and low volatility.

  10. Post-earnings drift

    Stocks that beat earnings expectations by a lot have tended to keep drifting in the same direction for weeks afterwards.

  11. Volatility selling

    Sell options to collect the premium, because implied volatility usually prices in more movement than actually happens. Small steady wins, rare large losses.

  12. Volatility targeting

    Not an entry signal but a sizing rule: hold less when the market is wild, more when it's calm, to keep risk steady. It improves almost every strategy above.

03 · Understand these concepts

To master probability.

Trading is betting with an edge, so probability is the language of the whole job. For each concept, simulate it in Python before moving on.

  1. Expected value

    The average outcome if you repeated a bet forever. A trade is only worth taking if this is positive after costs.

  2. Variance & standard deviation

    How spread out the outcomes are. In markets, standard deviation of returns is called volatility.

  3. Conditional probability

    The chance of A given that B already happened. "What happens after a 3% down day?" is a conditional question.

  4. Bayes' theorem

    How to update a belief when new evidence arrives. The core of how every good trader changes their mind.

  5. Independence

    Two events are independent if one tells you nothing about the other. Most "patterns" traders see fail this test.

  6. Normal distribution

    The bell curve. Many models assume returns follow it, which is useful and also wrong in the tails.

  7. Fat tails

    Extreme moves happen far more often than the bell curve predicts. The reason risk models blow up in crashes.

  8. Law of large numbers

    With enough repetitions, your average result approaches the expected value. Why one lucky trade proves nothing.

  9. Central limit theorem

    Averages of many random things look normal even when the things themselves don't. Why statistics works at all.

  10. Random walk

    A path built from random steps. The default assumption for prices, and the thing your strategy has to beat.

  11. Kelly criterion

    The bet size that grows money fastest given your edge. Most professionals bet a fraction of it, because Kelly is brutal when you overestimate your edge.

  12. Monte Carlo simulation

    Run thousands of random futures to see the range of outcomes, not just the average. The best tool for answering "how bad can it get?"

04 · Practice these functions

To master statistics.

Statistics is how you tell a real edge from luck. These are the actual Python functions quants use, from NumPy, SciPy, statsmodels and pandas. Install them with pip install numpy scipy statsmodels pandas yfinance.

  1. np.mean()

    Average return. Multiply daily by 252 to get a rough yearly figure.

  2. np.std()

    Volatility. Daily × √252 = annualised volatility.

  3. np.percentile()

    Value at risk: the 5th percentile of daily returns is your "bad day" number.

  4. np.corrcoef()

    Correlation between two assets, from −1 to +1.

  5. stats.skew()

    Are returns lopsided? Negative skew means occasional big losses, as with selling options.

  6. stats.kurtosis()

    Measures fat tails. Above 0 means more extreme days than a bell curve.

  7. stats.norm.ppf()

    Turns a probability into a z-score, e.g. 0.95 → 1.645. Used in risk limits.

  8. stats.ttest_1samp()

    Is the average return really different from zero, or is it noise?

  9. stats.jarque_bera()

    Tests whether returns are normally distributed. For real markets, they almost never are.

  10. stats.linregress()

    The slope of a stock against the market is its beta.

  11. sm.OLS()

    Full linear regression: how much of a return is explained by factors like market, size and value.

  12. adfuller()

    Tests stationarity: does the series wander off, or keep coming back to a mean? Mean reversion needs it.

  13. coint()

    Tests whether two prices are tied together over time. The foundation of pairs trading.

  14. acf()

    Autocorrelation: does today's return predict tomorrow's? Non-zero values hint at momentum or reversion.

  15. df.rolling()

    Rolling statistics: moving averages, rolling volatility and z-scores, the building blocks of most signals.

Try them on real data
import numpy as np
import yfinance as yf
from scipy import stats
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller, coint

px = yf.download(["KO", "PEP"], start="2018-01-01", auto_adjust=True)["Close"].dropna()
r = np.log(px).diff().dropna()

print("Annual volatility:\n", r.std() * np.sqrt(252))
print("5% daily VaR, KO:", np.percentile(r["KO"], 5))
print("Skew / kurtosis, KO:", stats.skew(r["KO"]), stats.kurtosis(r["KO"]))
print("Normal? Jarque-Bera p-value:", stats.jarque_bera(r["KO"]).pvalue)
print("KO price stationary? ADF p-value:", adfuller(px["KO"])[1])
print("KO/PEP cointegrated? p-value:", coint(px["KO"], px["PEP"])[1])

Spoiler: when I ran this in October 2026, Coke and Pepsi failed the cointegration test (p-value ≈ 1.0). The most famous textbook pair isn't one, which is exactly why you test instead of assuming. A p-value below 0.05 is the usual bar for "probably not luck". Test enough ideas and some will pass by chance, which is why the backtest traps in the project matter.

Build up to it in three levels:

  • 1

    Backtest. Pick two strategies from list 01 and backtest them with Claude Code, including fees and slippage. Test on data the strategy has never seen (walk-forward), not just the period you tuned it on.

  • 2

    Paper trade. Run the best one live on paper for at least four weeks. Compare live results to the backtest: the gap between them is the most honest number you will ever see.

  • 3

    Production. Build the 24/7 bot from the build guide. "Production level" means it runs without you: logs every decision, enforces risk limits in code, checks its positions against the exchange, restarts itself after crashes, and has a kill switch.

The traps that kill most backtests: look-ahead bias (using data you couldn't have had yet), survivorship bias (testing only stocks that still exist), overfitting (tuning until the past looks perfect), and ignoring fees. My AI desk once generated and backtested 180+ strategies; after fees, walk-forward testing and stress tests, exactly four survived. The full story is in the Trader Gauntlet.

Bonus · Interview questions

15 real quant interview questions.

These are the classic types that come up at trading firms: probability puzzles, expected value, mental maths and market sense. Try each one before opening the answer. Interviewers care about how you reason out loud more than the final number.

01You roll a fair die and I pay you the number that shows, in dollars. What's a fair price to play?EV
$3.50. The average of 1 to 6 is 21 ÷ 6 = 3.5.
02Same game, but after the first roll you may roll once more and take the second result instead. Fair price now?EV
$4.25. Re-roll only if the first roll is worth less than 3.5, so on 1, 2 or 3. Half the time you keep a 4, 5 or 6 (average 5); half the time you re-roll (average 3.5). 0.5 × 5 + 0.5 × 3.5 = 4.25.
03On average, how many rolls of a fair die does it take to get a 6?Probability
6. Each roll succeeds with probability 1/6, and the expected wait for an event with probability p is 1/p.
04You flip a fair coin until you get two heads in a row. Expected number of flips?Probability
6. Let E be the expected flips from the start. A tail (½) sends you back to the start; a head then a tail (¼) also does; two heads (¼) ends it. E = ½(1 + E) + ¼(2 + E) + ¼(2), so E = 6.
05A family has two children and at least one is a boy. What's the chance both are boys?Conditional
1/3. The equally likely options with at least one boy are BB, BG and GB. Only one of the three is BB.
06A disease affects 1% of people. A test is 99% accurate both ways. You test positive. Chance you have it?Bayes
50%. Out of 10,000 people, 100 are sick and 99 of them test positive. Of the 9,900 healthy people, 1% (99) also test positive. So 99 of the 198 positives are real.
07How many people do you need in a room for a better than 50% chance that two share a birthday?Probability
23. The chance that all birthdays differ falls below 50% at 23 people (ignoring leap years), because the number of pairs grows fast: 23 people make 253 pairs.
08Break a stick at two random points. What's the chance the three pieces can form a triangle?Geometry
1/4. A triangle needs every piece shorter than half the stick. Mapping the two break points onto a unit square, that region is exactly a quarter of the area.
09X and Y have correlation 0.9, and Y and Z have correlation 0.9. What's the lowest possible correlation between X and Z?Statistics
0.62. Think of correlations as cosines of angles. Each pair is about 25.8° apart, so X and Z can be at most twice that: cos(2θ) = 2 × 0.9² − 1 = 0.62.
10A bet pays even money and you win 60% of the time. What fraction of your bankroll should you bet?Kelly
20%. For even-money bets, Kelly says bet p − q = 0.6 − 0.4 = 0.2. In practice most traders bet half of that or less, because a slightly wrong edge estimate with full Kelly hurts a lot.
11A strategy has an annual Sharpe ratio of 1. What's its daily Sharpe?Markets
About 0.063. Sharpe scales with the square root of time, so divide by √252 ≈ 15.9.
12How many times a day do the hour and minute hands of a clock overlap?Brainteaser
22. The minute hand laps the hour hand 11 times every 12 hours, not 12, because the hour hand keeps moving. Twice a day makes 22.
13Quick: what is 17 × 23?Mental maths
391. One fast route: 20² − 3² = 400 − 9 = 391, since 17 × 23 = (20 − 3)(20 + 3).
14You backtested 1,000 strategies and the best one has a Sharpe of 2. Should you trade it?Research
Not yet. With 1,000 tries, some will look great by pure luck (the multiple testing problem). Test it on data it has never seen, check that it makes economic sense, and adjust for how many ideas you tried before believing it.
15What's the difference between correlation and cointegration?Statistics
Correlation is about returns moving together day to day. Cointegration is about prices staying tied together over the long run. Two stocks can be highly correlated yet drift apart forever, and pairs trading needs cointegration, not just correlation.
